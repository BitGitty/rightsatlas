Is Tarzan of the Apes public domain? The 1918 film is, unambiguously. But this is the clearest example on the site of why "public domain" is not the same as "free to use any way you like": the film is open, and the brand built on top of it is not. Check the layers below before commercial use.

Why the film print is free

The film opened in the US on January 27, 1918. Under the pre-1978 rules, its original 28-year term expired in 1946 and even a renewed second term would have expired no later than 1974. Every work published in the US in 1930 or earlier is now public domain by age, and this one cleared that bar decades ago. See the US Copyright Office's Circular 15a on duration.

The other layers

  • Story: public domain. The film adapts Edgar Rice Burroughs' novel, first published in All-Story Magazine in October 1912 and as a book in June 1914, both decades past their US term.
  • Score: not free. This is a silent film; whatever music a modern copy carries is a separate, later work.
  • Trademark: still protected, and this is the layer that matters here. Edgar Rice Burroughs, Inc., the company Burroughs founded to control the franchise, actively holds and enforces trademark rights in the Tarzan name and character. Trademark does not expire the way copyright does, and it survives the film's copyright entirely. Exhibiting or distributing this 1918 film needs no copyright license. Putting the Tarzan brand on merchandise, titles, or a new commercial project is a different question, and ERB Inc. is known to pursue it.
  • Restorations: not yet researched.

A little history

Directed by Scott Sidney and starring Elmo Lincoln as the adult Tarzan, with Gordon Griffith as the boy, this was the first film adaptation of Burroughs' novel, shot in Louisiana swamps in 1917. It was a commercial hit, earning about $1.5 million at the box office, and launched a franchise that produced three sequels with the same lead actors. Read more on Wikipedia.
