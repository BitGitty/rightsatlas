Is The Lost World public domain? Yes, the 1925 print is verified public domain in the US by term expiry. The novel it is based on is free too. Check the layers below before commercial use, because the dinosaur footage most people have actually watched comes from a restoration with its own added copyright.

Why the film print is free

First National Pictures published the film in 1925. Under the maximum 95-year US copyright term, it entered the public domain on January 1, 2021, regardless of whether a renewal was ever filed. Duke Law's Center for the Study of the Public Domain confirmed this on its Public Domain Day 2021 page.

The other layers

  • Story: public domain. The film adapts Arthur Conan Doyle's novel, first serialized in 1912. Under the 1909 Copyright Act's maximum 56-year term, the novel's US copyright would have expired by 1968 even with a timely renewal, well before this film's own term expired.
  • Score: not free on most copies. No synchronized score existed in 1925. Flicker Alley's 2017 restoration, with Lobster Films, added about ten minutes of newly found footage and a new orchestral score by Robert Israel; that score is a separately copyrighted new work, even though the restored images are not.
  • Trademark: not verified. We have not researched this layer yet.
  • Restorations: the 2017 Flicker Alley/Lobster Films restoration is the version most people will find, and it carries its own copyright in the new footage selection and Robert Israel's score. The underlying 1925 images stay as free as ever; what Flicker Alley added does not.

Archive.org hosts copies that predate that restoration under a public domain mark, but check the music on whichever copy you use.

A little history

Directed by Harry O. Hoyt and starring Wallace Beery as Professor Challenger, the film adapted Arthur Conan Doyle's 1912 novel for First National Pictures. Its stop-motion dinosaur effects, created by Willis O'Brien, combined animated creatures with live-action footage and directly established techniques O'Brien would later use on King Kong (1933).
