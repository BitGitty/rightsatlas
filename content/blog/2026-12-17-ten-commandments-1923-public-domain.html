Is the 1923 Ten Commandments public domain? Yes. Cecil B. DeMille's silent epic, not the better-known 1956 remake with Charlton Heston, is free to use in the US. For anyone building anything around the parting of the Red Sea, this older version is the one you can actually reuse.

Why the film print is free

The film opened in December 1923. Its US copyright term, 95 years from publication, has run out, and the Duke Center for the Study of the Public Domain lists 1923 works, including this one, as free in the US since January 1, 2019. Because the age-based term has already expired, no renewal search is needed to settle the question for a film this old. The film print is public domain.

The other layers to check

  • Score: partially protected. The film is silent. Whatever music is on your copy was added later and belongs to whoever recorded it; check the credits before you reuse it.
  • Story: public domain. The biblical prologue draws on the Book of Exodus, which is ancient and free, and Jeanie Macpherson's modern-day story was written for the film itself, so it carries the same 1923 status.
  • Trademark: not verified. We have not checked for any active marks tied to the franchise name.
  • Restorations: not verified. We have not checked what, if anything, later disc releases, such as the 2011 Blu-ray set, add to the original print.

A little history

DeMille directed the film for Famous Players-Lasky, and Paramount distributed it, with the premiere on December 4, 1923. Screenwriter Jeanie Macpherson built it in two parts: a biblical prologue on the Exodus, and a modern story about two brothers who respond differently to the commandments. The production filmed from May to August 1923 at several California locations, including the Guadalupe-Nipomo Dunes for the Egyptian scenes, and used groundbreaking Technicolor sequences for the biblical prologue, according to Wikipedia.
