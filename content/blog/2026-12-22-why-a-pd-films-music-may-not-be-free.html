Why a public domain film's music may not be free Charade (1963) fell into the US public domain the day it opened, but Henry Mancini's score never went with it. Can a film be public domain while its music is still protected? Yes, and Charade (1963) is the clearest US example we have on file. The picture and the score were never the same copyright, so one of them could fall into the public domain without taking the other with it.

The film print is free

Universal's own release prints of Charade carried a defective copyright notice, missing elements the 1909 Copyright Act required. A defective notice on publication put the work straight into the US public domain in 1963, with no renewal involved, because there was never a valid copyright to renew. This is one of the most-cited notice-failure cases in film-copyright literature, and the picture has circulated openly on the Internet Archive for two decades without a challenge to the images themselves. Full evidence: our Charade dossier.

The score is not

Henry Mancini's score, including the title song "Charade" with lyrics by Johnny Mercer, was registered and renewed as a musical work in its own right. That copyright never depended on the film print's notice, so it stayed intact even after the picture fell free. That is why "public domain" uploads of Charade still draw legitimate claims on the audio track: the claim is on the music, not the images.

The general rule

A film is really a bundle of separate copyrights: the picture itself, any underlying story or novel, and the music. Each one can expire, or never have existed, on its own schedule. A free film print does not clear the song playing over its own opening titles, and under the composition-versus-recording split, a free song does not clear a specific recording of it either.
