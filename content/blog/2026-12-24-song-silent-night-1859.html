Tonight millions of people will sing "Silent Night." Is it public domain? Yes, easily, and the story of how it was written is more interesting than most carols get.

Written in one night, out of necessity

Joseph Mohr wrote the German words in 1816. Two years later, on Christmas Eve 1818, he brought them to Franz Xaver Gruber and asked him to write a melody and guitar accompaniment for that very night's mass, because river flooding had possibly damaged the church organ. "Stille Nacht" was first performed hours later, at the Nikolauskirche in Oberndorf, Austria, according to Wikipedia. We wrote about this carol briefly before in our roundup of Christmas music and the public domain; here is the fuller story.

Which "Silent Night" is actually free, and since when

The German original from 1818 cleared the US public-domain-by-age bar long ago. But the version almost everyone in the US actually sings is a later English translation, published in 1859 by the Episcopal priest John Freeman Young. Our rule is to use the later date when a translation or new lyrics came after the tune, so this song's US status runs from 1859, which is still well before the 1930 cutoff for free-by-age works. Either date, it has been public domain in the US since 1 January 1935 at the latest.

A carol in about 300 languages

Wikipedia notes the carol has been translated into roughly 300 languages, likely more than almost any other Christmas song. Every one of those translations, and every modern recording of the carol, is its own separate copyright on its own clock; our explainer on song versus recording copyrights covers how that works. Bing Crosby's well-known recording with Paul Whiteman's orchestra is one such protected recording, apart from the free underlying carol.
