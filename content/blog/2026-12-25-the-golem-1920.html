Is The Golem (1920) public domain in the US? Yes. The German Expressionist horror classic went through copyright restoration and still came out free, because its restored term has already run out.

Why the film print is free

The Golem was published in Germany in October 1920. Germany is a Berne Convention country, so under the URAA (17 U.S.C. § 104A, effective 1 January 1996), the film's US copyright was restored on that date: it was still protected in Germany as of 1996 — co-director Carl Boese's life-plus-70 German term runs to 2028 — and had only fallen into the US public domain through missing US formalities such as registration or notice. But a restored US term still runs only 95 years from publication: 1920 plus 95 is 2015. So the print has been public domain in the US since 1 January 2016 either way, the same cutoff point Duke's Center for the Study of the Public Domain confirms for the equivalent 95-year class of works.

The other layers

The story is free too. The screenplay by Paul Wegener and Henrik Galeen was first published with the 1920 film, so its US term also expired in 2015. The source novel, Gustav Meyrink's Der Golem (1915), is listed by Project Gutenberg as public domain in the US, and the Golem legend itself is ancient Jewish folklore with no copyright at all.

The score is the layer to watch. The Golem was shown silent, with live accompaniment, and no synchronized recording was ever made for it. Every commercial release bundles a newly commissioned score instead: the 2018 Friedrich-Wilhelm-Murnau-Stiftung restoration, on Kino Lorber (US) and Eureka Video (UK) Blu-ray, pairs the film with four separately owned scores, by Stephen Horne, Admir Shkurtaj, Lukasz "Wudec" Poleszak and Cordula Heth. None of those are free. A faithful restoration like that one adds no new copyright to the images themselves, but ripping a copy-protected disc can still run into the DMCA's anti-circumvention rule. Trademark status was left undetermined; no search for live marks has been done.

A little history

Directed by Paul Wegener and Carl Boese, the film stars Wegener as a towering clay figure summoned by a rabbi to defend Prague's Jewish ghetto. Hans Poelzig's cave-like sets and the shadow-heavy cinematography of Karl Freund and Guido Seeber gave it a visual style that James Whale's Frankenstein would echo a decade later.
