Is The Man Who Laughs public domain? Yes, since 1 January 2024. Conrad Veidt's fixed grin inspired the look of the Joker, but that comic-book connection has nothing to do with why you can use this film.

Is The Man Who Laughs free to use?

Universal Pictures published The Man Who Laughs in 1928. Its 95-year term expired on 31 December 2023, and Duke Law's Public Domain Day 2024 page lists it among that year's films. We also checked the 1955, 1956 and 1957 Catalog of Copyright Entries motion-picture volumes, 1956 being the 28th-year renewal window, and found no renewal entry, which backs up the same result.

What about the music in The Man Who Laughs?

  • Score: partially protected. The 1928 Movietone score, built around the song "When Love Comes Stealing" by Ernő Rapée, was part of the film's own unrenewed copyright and is public domain. But the widely circulated 2019 4K restoration carries a new score performed by the Berklee College of Music Silent Film Orchestra, which is separately protected. Look for a copy with the original 1928 score, or add your own music.
  • Story: verified public domain. The film adapts Victor Hugo's 1869 novel "L'Homme qui rit," which has been free for well over a century.
  • Trademark: not verified.
  • Restorations: partially protected. Kino International's 2007 restoration, the 2019 Berklee-scored 4K release, and a 2020 Masters of Cinema edition all add their own new material. A faithful restoration itself adds no new copyright, but a new score or colorization does.

What's the story behind The Man Who Laughs?

Paul Leni directed The Man Who Laughs for Universal, with Conrad Veidt as Gwynplaine, a nobleman whose face was carved into a permanent smile as a child, and Mary Philbin as the blind Dea who loves him. It premiered at New York's Central Theatre on 27 April 1928. Universal spent over a million dollars on the production, and makeup artist Jack Pierce built Gwynplaine's grin, which later shaped the visual design of DC's Joker, though DC's trademark on that character covers comics and media, not this 1928 film.
