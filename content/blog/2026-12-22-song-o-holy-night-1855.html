"O Holy Night" is one of the most dramatic carols ever written, and the English version everyone sings is a translation of a translation. Here is what we found about its US copyright.

Is the song "O Holy Night" in the public domain?

"O Holy Night," in the English words sung today, is public domain in the US. Those words were published in 1855, so the song became free on 1 January 1931 at the latest. Anyone can now record it, perform it, or print the sheet music without permission.

When was "O Holy Night" published?

Wikipedia lays out the chain: Placide Cappeau wrote the French poem "Cantique de Noel" in 1847, and Adolphe Adam set it to music the same year. The English words most people know are a separate work: American writer John Sullivan Dwight's adaptation, published in 1855. Because that English version is the later work, we use 1855 for its US status. A song published in year Y is free in the US from 1 January of Y + 96 at the latest; for 1855 that is 1931.

Can you use a recording of "O Holy Night"?

The carol's words and tune are free, but a specific performance of it is a separate, protected copyright. A famous choir or soloist's recording of "O Holy Night" stays protected even though the song itself does not.

"O Holy Night": where do people know it from?

The carol is often said to be the first song ever broadcast by radio, in Reginald Fessenden's 1906 Christmas Eve violin broadcast, though Wikipedia notes this claim is not formally documented (source).
