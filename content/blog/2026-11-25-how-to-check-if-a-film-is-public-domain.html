"It's public domain" is one of the most repeated claims about old films, and one of the most often wrong. You can check most US cases yourself, for free, in four steps. This is the same method we use on every page of this site.

Step 1: find the year it was published

US copyright counts from publication (or registration), not from when a film was shot or when it premiered somewhere. Look for the copyright date on the registration record, not the year on a poster. Two famous "1931" films, Little Caesar and Cimarron, were registered in December 1930, so they became free a year earlier than most people expected. More on that trap here.

  • Published 1930 or earlier: public domain in the US by age. Done (but read step 4).
  • Published 1931 to 1963: go to step 2.
  • Published 1964 to 1977: go to step 3.
  • Published 1978 or later: almost certainly still protected.

Step 2 (1931 to 1963): was the copyright renewed?

Works from these years had a first term of 28 years. To keep the copyright, the owner had to file a renewal in the 28th year. Many never did, and those works fell into the public domain.

Renewals are printed in the Catalog of Copyright Entries (CCE), which the Copyright Office published every year. Scans of the volumes are free on the Internet Archive. For a film published in 1936, look in the 1963 and 1964 volumes for motion pictures, in the renewal section. Renewals filed from 1978 onward are also in the Copyright Office's online public records.

No renewal found in the right year means the film print is likely free. A renewal found means it is still protected (it now runs 95 years from publication).

Step 3 (1964 to 1977): was there a copyright notice?

For works from 1964 on, renewal became automatic, so a missing renewal changes nothing. What matters is the notice: before 1978, a work published without a proper copyright notice (©, "Copyright" or "Copr.", the year and the owner) could lose protection. You need to see the original release prints or a reliable source about them.

Step 4: check every layer, not just the film

Even when the film print is free, other parts may not be:

  • The story: a film based on a novel or short story can still be controlled through the story's copyright.
  • The music: the score and songs often have their own copyrights.
  • Foreign films: many had their US copyright restored on 1 January 1996 (the URAA), even if they had lost it before.
  • Restorations and colorized versions: new work added later can have its own copyright.
  • Trademarks: famous characters and logos can be trademarks, which never expire while they are used.
