Is Nosferatu public domain? People ask this a lot, especially since the 2024 remake put the name back in the news. The short answer: yes. The 1922 film print is public domain in the US, and so is the novel it stole from. The part people get wrong is the sound, not the picture.

Why the film print is free

Nosferatu was made in Germany in 1922, which makes people assume it needs a special foreign-film check. It doesn't, here. Even the 1996 URAA restoration, which gave some foreign works a second US copyright, cannot restore a work whose full US term has already run out. Works published before 1923 maxed out at a 75-year US term, so the term from 1922 ran out no later than 1 January 1998, and the print is verified public domain in the US regardless of its German origin (Cornell Copyright Term chart). As a general rule, any US-released film published in 1930 or earlier is free by age; a film from a foreign country that old needs this slightly longer check, but the answer here is the same.

The other layers

  • Story: verified public domain. Nosferatu is an unauthorized adaptation of Bram Stoker's Dracula (1897). The novel has been free for decades.
  • Score: partially protected. Hans Erdmann's 1922 score is free as sheet music, but almost every recording you can actually get is a modern performance with its own copyright. Treat the audio on any copy as protected unless you can prove otherwise.
  • Restorations: partially protected. A faithful scan of the public-domain print adds no new US copyright. A newly composed score, colorization, or new subtitles on a specific restoration can be protected on their own.
  • Trademark: not verified. The 2024 Eggers remake is an active studio property. Avoid marketing that implies a connection to it, and avoid using its branding, poster art, or title design on anything you sell.

A little history

Prana Film shot Nosferatu in Germany and Slovakia in 1921 without buying the rights to Dracula, and it premiered in Berlin on 4 March 1922. Bram Stoker's widow sued and won a court order to destroy every print. Copies survived anyway, and the film is now considered one of the most influential horror films ever made (Wikipedia).
