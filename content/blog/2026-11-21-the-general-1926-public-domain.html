People search "is The General public domain" because it is routinely named one of the greatest films ever made, and because silent-film copyright is rarely simple. Here it is simple: the film print is public domain in the US, verified from a primary source. Check the layers below before commercial use.

Why the film print is free

The General was published in 1926. Its 95-year US copyright term expired on January 1, 2022, which places it in the public domain by age regardless of any renewal history. Duke Law's Center for the Study of the Public Domain lists it among the works that entered the public domain that year: see the Public Domain Day 2022 list. The film is also widely reported to have lapsed earlier through a missed copyright renewal, but the age rule alone already settles the question.

The other layers

  • Story: public domain. The film is based on William Pittenger's 1863 memoir of the Great Locomotive Chase, long out of copyright.
  • Score: not free. The General is a silent film, so the music on any modern copy, disc, or streaming release is a separate recording made decades later. That recording is protected on its own, even though the images are not.
  • Trademark: no active mark is known tied to the title, but we have not run a full trademark search, so treat this as likely rather than verified.
  • Restorations: a faithful restoration of the images adds no new US copyright. What a restoration adds, such as a newly recorded score, colorization, or new title cards, can be protected on its own, and ripping a copy-protected disc can raise DMCA anti-circumvention problems even when the film itself is free.

The safest source is an archival scan, such as the copies on the Internet Archive, rather than a branded disc release. Replace the audio with your own score or a licensed one.

A little history

Buster Keaton directed The General with Clyde Bruckman on a $750,000 budget, filming in Cottage Grove, Oregon with around 1,500 local extras. Critics dismissed it as "far from funny" on its February 1927 release; decades later Orson Welles called it "perhaps the greatest film ever made," and it was chosen for the first class of the National Film Registry in 1989.
