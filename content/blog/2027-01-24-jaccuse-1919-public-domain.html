Is J'accuse! public domain? Yes, and the way it got there is a good example of how URAA restoration works. The French print is public domain in the US today, but only because its restored term has since run out, not because it never had one. Check the layers below before commercial use.

Why the film print is free

J'accuse! was first published in France in April 1919. A US copyright on a 1919 work carries a maximum 95-year term, expiring January 1, 2015, and 1919 sits well inside the pre-1930 bright line for US public domain by age. As a French work, it was also eligible for URAA restoration: director Abel Gance died in 1981, and the film was still protected in France on January 1, 1996, which would have restored its US copyright under 17 U.S.C. 104A. But URAA restoration only runs for the remainder of the same 95-year term counted from 1919, and that term had already expired by January 1, 2015. The print is public domain by exhaustion of that restored term. See US Copyright Office Circular 38b on URAA restoration.

The other layers

  • Story: public domain. Abel Gance wrote an original scenario; there is no underlying novel or play to clear, and the screenplay shares the film's own 1919 term.
  • Score: not free on the version most people will find. The original print is silent and carries no recorded score. Flicker Alley's 2018 release pairs the restored print with a newly commissioned symphonic score by Robert Israel, and that recording is not cleared by the print's public domain status.
  • Trademark: not verified.
  • Restorations: a 2007 reconstruction by Nederlands Filmmuseum and Lobster Films assembled the film from six surviving prints, and Flicker Alley's 2018 release adds the Robert Israel score on top of that. Both incorporate protected editorial or audio choices that are not cleared just because the underlying 1919 images are free.

An unscored print is viewable at the Internet Archive, which is the cleanest source if you plan to add your own audio.

A little history

Directed by Abel Gance and released in France on April 25, 1919, the film starred Romuald Joube and Severin-Mars and was financed by Charles Pathe, with production running from August 1918 to March 1919. Gance filmed actual battle footage during the Battle of Saint-Mihiel in September 1918 and worked it into the film's final sections; after its 1920 London premiere, Pathe's London agent told Gance his name there was "more famous than Griffith's."
