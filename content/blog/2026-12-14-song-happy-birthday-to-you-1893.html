For decades, people were told "Happy Birthday to You" was under copyright and that singing it in a film or ad could cost money. A US court finally settled the question, and the answer is: it is free.

The short answer

"Happy Birthday to You" is public domain in the US. This is not just a case of an old song outliving its copyright term; a federal court looked at the actual claim and ruled it invalid.

The evidence

Wikipedia explains the history: Mildred and Patty Hill published the tune "Good Morning to All" in 1893. The "Happy Birthday to You" lyrics first appeared in print in 1912, with no copyright notice and no credit to any author. Warner/Chappell later claimed a 1935 copyright covered the song, but on 22 September 2015 a federal judge ruled that the 1935 copyright covered only one piano arrangement, not the lyrics or the melody. On 30 June 2016, the court formally declared the song to be in the public domain.

Recordings are a different right

The song's words and tune are free, but a specific recording or a new arrangement of it is still its own, separate copyright. A film score's orchestral version of "Happy Birthday" can still be protected even though the song underneath it is not.

Where people know it from

It barely needs an introduction: per the 1998 Guinness World Records, it is the most recognized song in the English language (source).
