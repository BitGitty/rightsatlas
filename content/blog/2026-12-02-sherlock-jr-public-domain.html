Is Sherlock Jr. public domain? Buster Keaton's 1924 comedy, about a film projectionist who dreams himself into the movie he's screening, is one of the most-reused silent films online, and yes, the picture itself is free to use in the US.

Why the film print is free

Sherlock Jr. was released on 21 April 1924. The US copyright term for a 1924 work runs 95 years and expired at the end of 2019, so the film print became verified public domain on 1 January 2020 (Internet Archive item metadata, Public Domain Mark 1.0). That is the same bright line that frees every other US film from 1929 or earlier once its particular anniversary arrives; 1924 films cleared it on that date along with the rest of that year's releases.

The other layers

  • Story: verified public domain. It is an original screenplay by Clyde Bruckman, Jean Havez and Joseph A. Mitchell, not an adaptation, so it shares the film's own public-domain status.
  • Score: partially protected. The film is silent. Later composers, including Club Foot Orchestra, Craig Marks, Timothy Brock and Robert Israel, each recorded their own accompaniment, and each one is a separate, protected work.
  • Restorations: partially protected. Modern releases may add a new score or new title cards. We have not confirmed which specific restoration you would be sourcing from, so check each release's credits before reuse.
  • Trademark: not verified. The "Sherlock" in the title is Keaton's amateur detective, not Conan Doyle's, but it still trips a franchise flag worth checking before branded use, especially if you plan to sell merchandise rather than just reuse the footage.

A little history

Keaton directed and starred in Sherlock Jr., shooting it in Los Angeles in early 1924 under the working title "The Misfit." It earned $448,337 at the box office on release, a rare failure in his career at the time. The Library of Congress added it to the National Film Registry in 1991 as culturally significant (Wikipedia).
