A song and its recording are two different copyrights US law treats a composition and a sound recording as separate copyrights with separate clocks. If a song is public domain, is every recording of it free too? No. US law treats the composition (the melody and lyrics, as written or published) and a sound recording (one specific performance, captured on disc or tape) as two separate copyrights. Each one runs on its own clock, and they rarely end on the same date.

Two different terms

A composition published in the US in 1923 or later follows the ordinary rule for print: 95 years from publication, then free. (A composition published before 1923 maxed out earlier, at a flat 75-year term, and was already free by 1998 at the latest.) We track eighteen of them, all first published in 1931, becoming free on 1 January 2027.

Sound recordings did not even have federal copyright protection until 1972, and recordings made before that date were covered by a patchwork of state laws. The Music Modernization Act of 2018 brought these older recordings into the federal system and set a public-domain schedule for them, confirmed by the US Copyright Office's Classics Protection and Access Act page.

The schedule that matters right now

Recordings first published before 1923 are already public domain. Recordings first published between 1923 and 1946 get federal protection for 100 years from publication, then become public domain on 1 January of the following year. A recording made in 1926 turns 100 years old in 2026, so it becomes public domain on 1 January 2027 — the same date as our 1931 compositions, by coincidence of the arithmetic, not because it is the same rule.

Why this trips people up

A famous old song and a famous old recording of it often get mixed up as "the same thing turning free." They are not. The 1931 songs becoming free next year are compositions only. The specific 1931 recordings people actually remember — Crosby singing, Calloway singing — are separate recording copyrights, most of them still protected today, and they will reach the public domain on their own 100-year schedule, not the song's 95-year one.
