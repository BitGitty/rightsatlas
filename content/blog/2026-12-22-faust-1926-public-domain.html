Is Faust (1926) public domain? Yes, the US copyright on F. W. Murnau's film expired on January 1, 2022. The legend and the German novel behind it are long free too. Check the layers below before commercial use, because the restoration most people will actually find is not as free as the original images.

Why the film print is free

Faust was published in 1926. Its maximum 95-year US copyright term expired December 31, 2021, placing it in the public domain from January 1, 2022, and Duke Law's Center for the Study of the Public Domain lists it among the films that entered that year: see the Public Domain Day 2022 page. Faust is a German production by UFA, so URAA restoration was possible in theory, but URAA can only restore the same 95-year term, which has also already expired. No active restoration remains.

The other layers

  • Story: public domain. The film adapts Goethe's Faust, Part I (1808) and Part II (1832), both decades out of copyright, and Hans Kyser's 1926 screenplay shares the film's own expired term.
  • Score: not free on the copies most people will find. Faust had no recorded soundtrack in 1926. Kino Lorber's Blu-ray carries a piano score by Javier Perez de Azpeitia, an ensemble score by the Mont Alto Motion Picture Orchestra, and Timothy Brock's 1995 orchestral score on the US release version. Those recordings are protected; a score you record yourself is yours.
  • Trademark: not verified. "Faust" is a centuries-old literary name used across countless works, and no film-specific mark is known, but a US Trademark Electronic Search System check is worth doing before a commercial project.
  • Restorations: the F. W. Murnau Foundation restored the German release version from 35mm elements, and that restoration is what appears on Kino Lorber's disc, Eureka's UK release, and most streaming copies. The restored images add no new copyright; the scores layered onto that restoration do.

A little history

Directed by F. W. Murnau and produced by Erich Pommer at UFA's Neubabelsberg studios, the film starred Gosta Ekman as Faust and Emil Jannings as Mephisto, drawing on Goethe and on Christopher Marlowe's Doctor Faustus. It took six months to shoot at a cost of 2 million Reichsmark, and it was the last film Murnau completed before leaving for Hollywood. Read more on Wikipedia.
