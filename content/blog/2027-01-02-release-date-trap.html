The reason is a registration date, and it can catch you in both directions. release-date-trap.jpg | RightsAtlas title card: The release-date trap, 1930 vs 1931

Yesterday the films of 1931 entered the US public domain. But two famous "1931" films were not on the list, because they were already free: Little Caesar and Cimarron, the Best Picture winner. Here is why, and how to avoid the same mistake with other films.

What happened

Little Caesar was released on 9 January 1931, and Cimarron came out early in 1931. But both were registered for copyright in December 1930. For these films the copyright term counts from that 1930 date, not from the premiere. So their 95 years ended at the end of 2025, and both entered the US public domain on 1 January 2026.

A reader on Reddit's r/publicdomain pointed this out to us, and we corrected our Class of 1931 page. Thank you.

Why it matters

  • It can make a film free earlier, as with these two.
  • It can also mislead you the other way. A film shown at a festival or premiere one year may not have been "published" until a later general release. If you count from the earlier date, you may think a film is free when it is not.

The rule

Find the date on the copyright record: the registration in the Catalog of Copyright Entries, or the notice on the film itself. The year on a poster, a database or a streaming page is a hint, not proof. Our step-by-step guide shows where to look.
