Is The Terror public domain? Yes. Roger Corman's quick, chaotic 1963 production never carried a copyright notice when it was released, and under the law of the time that omission alone put it in the public domain.

Is The Terror free to use?

The Terror (1963). The Filmgroup released the film through American International Pictures on June 17, 1963, with no copyright notice on any print. Under the 1909 Copyright Act, which governed US works through 1977, publishing without a notice was an irrevocable dedication to the public domain. We checked the 1963 and 1964 Catalog of Copyright Entries, Motion Pictures and Filmstrips volumes, and found no registration for The Terror in either one, even though other AIP titles from the same season, such as Black Sabbath and The Comedy of Terrors, do appear. What about the music in The Terror?

  • Story: public domain. Leo Gordon and Jack Hill wrote an original screenplay for the film, with no outside source novel or play, and no separate registration turned up in either catalog.
  • Score: partly protected. Ronald Stein composed the score, with uncredited cues likely contributed by Les Baxter. The recorded track in the film shares the print's public-domain status, since it was published as part of the same unregistered, notice-less work. Baxter, however, routinely registered his own compositions separately, and whether any specific cue here was separately renewed has not been verified.
  • Restorations: partly protected. Film Chest's 2011 DVD added a newly engineered 5.1 surround mix, which is a separately protected recording. A 1990 international re-edit released as The Haunting added new framing scenes specifically to create a new copyright and is not public domain.
  • Trademark: not verified. We found no known franchise or merchandising program tied to the film, but we did not search USPTO records.

What's the story behind The Terror?

According to Wikipedia, Roger Corman directed The Terror with Boris Karloff as a reclusive baron and Jack Nicholson as a Napoleonic-era soldier who stumbles onto his castle and a shapeshifting demon. Corman planned it as a quick two-day shoot on leftover sets from The Raven, but the production stretched to nine months and passed through several directors, including Francis Ford Coppola and Monte Hellman, before it was finished.
