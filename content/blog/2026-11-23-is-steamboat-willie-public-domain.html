Is Steamboat Willie public domain? Yes. The 1928 cartoon that introduced Mickey Mouse entered the US public domain on January 1, 2024, when its 95-year copyright term ran out. That makes the film free to copy, remix and sell in the US. "Mickey Mouse" the brand is a different question, and that is where most people trip up.

Why the film print is free

Steamboat Willie (1928). Walt Disney released it on November 18, 1928. Works published in the US in 1928 get a maximum 95-year copyright term, which expired at the end of 2023. The Duke Center for the Study of the Public Domain calls it "the most publicized Public Domain Day entry in history" and lists it among the works that entered the US public domain on January 1, 2024, soundtrack included. See the Duke CSPD Public Domain Day 2024 page.

The other layers: trademark is the trap

  • Score: public domain. The 1928 synchronized soundtrack is part of the published 1928 work and expired with it. A modern re-recording of the same cues would be a separate, newer copyright.
  • Story: public domain. It is an original 1928 short with no separate underlying work to clear.
  • Trademark: still protected. Disney holds live, actively enforced trademarks in the Mickey Mouse name and image as brand identifiers (see USPTO trademark records). You can use the 1928 film and its 1928 character design creatively. You cannot put that same mouse on a logo, a product label, or anything that implies your project comes from Disney. The later, redesigned Mickey (gloves, color, rounder proportions) is also still under copyright, separately from this 1928 version.
  • Restorations: partly protected. A faithful restoration of the public-domain frames adds no new copyright. What a restorer adds — a new score, color, new titles — can be protected on its own, and Disney's own cleaned-up masters are a separate, protected asset. Ripping a copy-protected disc can also trigger the DMCA's anti-circumvention rule regardless of the film's status.

A little history

Walt Disney and Ub Iwerks made the cartoon for Disney's own studio, and Pat Powers' Celebrity Productions released it on November 18, 1928, with one of animation's first synchronized soundtracks. According to Wikipedia, the title may be a parody of Buster Keaton's Steamboat Bill, Jr., released the same year.
