Is "Mood Indigo" public domain? Not yet, but on 1 January 2027 at the latest, the song becomes free. The date that matters is 1931, not 1930, and the gap between the two years shows how these old standards were actually made.

Recorded first, published later

Duke Ellington's band first played the tune for a radio broadcast in October 1930, under the title "Dreamy Blues," according to Wikipedia's account of the song. The song as "Mood Indigo," with Irving Mills's lyrics added, was published and copyrighted in 1931 by Mills Music. We confirmed that date directly on the original sheet music's own catalog record at the UW-Madison Mills Music Library, which lists the published sheet music as 1931.

Why the gap matters

A recording and a composition are different copyrights with different clocks; see our explainer on why a song and a recording are different copyrights. Ellington's October 1930 broadcast performance as "Dreamy Blues" is its own, separately-timed recording copyright. The composition — the tune and Mills's lyrics as published in 1931 — is what follows the ordinary 95-year rule for print, ending its term on 31 December 2026 and becoming public domain on 1 January 2027 at the latest.

Where you have heard it

It became Duke Ellington's signature tune, recorded and performed by his orchestra for decades afterward (Wikipedia). Every one of those later recordings is its own copyright, on its own schedule, separate from the 1931 song itself.

What is actually free on 1 January 2027

Only the composition: the tune credited to Duke Ellington and Barney Bigard, with Irving Mills's lyrics, exactly as published by Mills Music in 1931. Arrangements, later recordings, and any newer lyrics or translations are separate works with their own copyright status. Before using a specific recording commercially, check that recording's own status, not just the song's.
