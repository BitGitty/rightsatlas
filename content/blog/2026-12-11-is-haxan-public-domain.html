Is Häxan public domain? Yes. Benjamin Christensen's 1922 half-documentary about the history of witchcraft is free to use in the US, no matter which country it came from.

Why the film print is free

Häxan was published in Sweden in 1922. Any work published before 1923 is free in the US by age, and the Duke Center for the Study of the Public Domain confirms the longest possible US term for a 1922 work, 75 years, ran out on January 1, 1998. Because the film was already free well before the URAA took effect in 1996, that law, which can restore some foreign copyrights, had nothing left to restore. The film print is public domain.

The other layers

  • Score: partially protected. Häxan was shot silent. Every circulating copy we know of, including streaming and home video, pairs the images with a score added after the fact, and that score is a separate, newer work. Only an unscored transfer, such as the one on the Internet Archive, avoids that problem.
  • Story: public domain. The story is Christensen's own, first published with the 1922 film, so it shares the film's status.
  • Trademark: not verified. We have not checked for any active marks.
  • Restorations: partially protected. A faithful transfer of the original print adds no new copyright, but several releases add things of their own, including a 1968 reissue re-edited by British filmmaker Antony Balch with narration by William S. Burroughs. Check what a specific copy adds before you reuse it.

A little history

Director Benjamin Christensen made Häxan for Svensk Filmindustri, shooting it in Denmark in 1920 and 1921. The film traces the history of witchcraft from the Middle Ages onward, and argues that many of the women once burned as witches were likely suffering from conditions we would now call mental or neurological illness. It used detailed period recreations and early special effects, including puppetry and stop-motion, according to Wikipedia.
