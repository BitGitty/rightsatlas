Night of the Living Dead (1968): public domain because of one title change Night of the Living Dead lost its US copyright before release, after a title change stripped the notice from every print. Is Night of the Living Dead public domain? Yes. George A. Romero's 1968 zombie film lost its US copyright before it ever reached a cinema, and the reason is one of the strangest accidents in American film history: a last-minute title change.

Why the film print is free

The film was shot under the title Night of the Flesh Eaters. When distributor Walter Reade Organization changed the title to Night of the Living Dead just before release, the copyright notice card was not updated to match, and the theatrical prints went out in 1968 with no copyright notice at all. US Copyright Office Circular 22 explains the rule that applied at the time: "as a general rule under the 1909 law, copyright protection was lost permanently if the notice was omitted from the first authorized published edition of a work." There was no cure window for a 1968 film, so the loss was immediate and permanent.

This is backed up by the absence of any registration. A search of the 1968 Catalog of Copyright Entries — the annual motion-picture registration volume covering the year of release — turned up no entry for "Night of the Living Dead," "Night of the Flesh Eaters," or the production company Image Ten. No one ever registered a copyright for this film.

The other layers

The screenplay, by Romero and John Russo, was never published separately. It reached the public only through the noticeless film prints, so it is public domain for the same reason as the print.

The music is the real trap. The recorded soundtrack as released in 1968 is public domain along with everything else in the prints. But the score was assembled from pre-existing Hi-Q production library cues, written by composers including Spencer Moore, William Loose, Geordie Hormel and Ib Glindemann. Those underlying compositions were separately registered, some as early as 1956, and the current Hi-Q catalog holder can still assert a claim if you pull the audio out of the film and use it on its own. Using the film whole, picture and sound together, is safe; lifting just the music is not.

Trademark in the title was not confirmed either way. A faithful restoration of a public-domain film does not create new copyright in the pictures, but Criterion's 2018 Blu-ray restoration may carry its own new sound mix or newly designed titles, and ripping a copy-protected disc can break the DMCA's anti-circumvention rule even though the film itself is free. Use an Internet Archive copy of the unrestored 1968 print instead.

A little history

Romero directed the film independently for Image Ten Productions on a budget of around $114,000, shooting largely in rural Pennsylvania. It became the film that set the template for the modern zombie movie.
