Christmas films people call "public domain": what we could verify, and the traps Which Christmas classics are really free in the US? Two we verified from primary sources, and three famous ones that are not as simple as people think. christmas-carol-1910.jpg | Film still from the 1910 Edison film A Christmas Carol, showing Scrooge with a ghost

Above: A Christmas Carol (Edison, 1910). Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.

Every December the same Christmas classics get uploaded, remixed and sold as "public domain". We check US status film by film, layer by layer. Here is what we could verify from primary sources, and what we could not.

Verified public domain (US)

A Christmas Carol (1910). Published in 1910, so it is free by age. In the US, everything published in 1930 or earlier is now public domain.

Christmas Comes But Once a Year (1936, Fleischer). Released 4 December 1936, so its renewal was due in 1964. We read the 1964 Catalog of Copyright Entries (motion pictures volume), renewal section: there is no renewal for this title, or for any Fleischer Color Classics title.

Often called public domain, but not verified

It's a Wonderful Life (1946). The film print is widely reported to have lapsed in 1974 after a missed renewal. We have not confirmed that from the 1974 catalog ourselves yet. And even if the print is free, the underlying story ("The Greatest Gift") and the music are separate rights. That is how the owners took control of the film again in the 1990s. A free print is not the same as a free film.

Scrooge (1951, Alastair Sim). A British film. It opened in the US as "A Christmas Carol" a few days after its UK premiere. Whether its US copyright was restored in 1996 (under the URAA) depends on that timing, and on whether a theatrical release counts as "publication". It is unresolved, so we don't call it free.

Santa Claus Conquers the Martians (1964). A missing renewal cannot make a 1964 film public domain, because renewal became automatic for works from 1964 on. It could only be free if the 1964 prints had no copyright notice, and we could not verify that from any source.

The quick rule

  • Published 1930 or earlier: free by age.
  • Published 1931 to 1963: free only if the copyright was not renewed in its 28th year. Renewals are listed in the Catalog of Copyright Entries, scanned on the Internet Archive.
  • Published 1964 to 1977: renewal was automatic, so only a missing copyright notice can make it free.
  • Always check the story, the music and any restoration separately.
