Is The Jazz Singer public domain? Yes, in the US, since 1 January 2023, and unlike most silent-to-sound transition films, its original soundtrack is free too. The catch is in the modern copies that circulate today.

Why the film print, and the sound, are free

Warner Bros. published The Jazz Singer on 6 October 1927. Under US Copyright Office Circular 15a, the maximum term for a 1927 work is 95 years, which expired on 31 December 2022, putting the film into the public domain the next day. Because the Vitaphone synchronized recordings, Al Jolson's singing and his spoken lines, were created as part of the 1927 production, they share that same expired term. The songs performed on screen, including Irving Berlin's "Blue Skies" (1926) and "My Mammy" (1920), have also expired by their own terms.

The other layers

  • Story: verified public domain. The screenplay adapts Samson Raphaelson's 1922 short story "The Day of Atonement" and his 1925 play of the same name. Both expired before the film did, on 1 January 2018 and 1 January 2021.
  • Trademark: not verified.
  • Restorations: partially protected. Kino Lorber and Warner Bros. Home Entertainment have released restored editions on DVD and Blu-ray, and any newly recorded score or colorization on those discs is separately protected, even though a faithful restoration itself adds no new copyright. A little history

Alan Crosland directed The Jazz Singer for Warner Bros., starring Al Jolson, May McAvoy and Warner Oland. It premiered on 6 October 1927 at Warner's flagship New York theater, timed for the evening before Yom Kippur to match the film's story about a cantor's son. Each of Jolson's musical numbers was recorded on a separate reel with its own sound disc, a genuine technical challenge for projectionists at the time.
