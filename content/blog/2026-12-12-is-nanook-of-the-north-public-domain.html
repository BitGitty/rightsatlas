Is Nanook of the North public domain? Yes. Robert Flaherty's 1922 documentary was never renewed, and its term expired decades ago. The images are free. What usually isn't free is the music layered on top in later editions.

Why the film print is free

Nanook of the North (1922). Pathé Exchange released the film in the US on June 11, 1922, for producer Revillon Frères. Under the 1909 Copyright Act, a work from 1922 had a 28-year initial term plus an optional 28-year renewal, for a maximum of 56 years. That renewal window closed in 1950. Neither Revillon Frères nor Pathé Exchange, which went through bankruptcy and reorganization by the early 1930s, filed a renewal, so the film's US copyright expired no later than 1950. See US Copyright Office Circular 15a on duration of copyright.

The other layers: the score is the trap

  • Story: public domain. Flaherty filmed real Inuit life and did not adapt an existing protected work, so the story shares the print's expired term.
  • Score: partly protected. The 1922 print is silent and carries no original soundtrack. Almost every copy in circulation adds one anyway: Stanley Silverman composed a 1973 orchestral score for an educational release, and Gabriel Thibaudeau wrote a new score for the 2014 Flicker Alley Blu-ray restoration. Either score is separately copyrighted; lifting one without clearance infringes it even though the film itself is free.
  • Restorations: partly protected. A faithful restoration of public-domain images adds no new US copyright. The 2014 Flicker Alley/Lobster Films Blu-ray and the Criterion Eclipse Series 33 release each pair the restored image with a protected score and other new material.
  • Trademark: not verified. We have not checked this layer.

A little history

According to Wikipedia, the film follows an Inuit man credited as Nanook and his family in Canada's Arctic, shot by Flaherty from August 1920 to August 1921 in the Ungava Peninsula of Quebec. It became the first feature-length documentary to achieve wide commercial success, and the US Library of Congress selected it for the National Film Registry in 1989.
